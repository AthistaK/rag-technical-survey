Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): A Technical Survey

Author: ATHISTA K   |   Date: August 2026

Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) can generate fluent and useful text, but their knowledge is limited by training data, may become outdated, and can sometimes produce unsupported statements. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses these limitations by connecting a language model to an external knowledge source. Instead of relying only on information stored in model parameters, a RAG system retrieves relevant documents or passages and provides them to the generator as context. This article surveys the foundations of RAG, its architecture, retrieval methods, major research developments, advantages, limitations, evaluation considerations, and practical applications.

1. Introduction

Generative AI systems have changed how users search for information, write content, and interact with software. However, a conventional LLM answers primarily from patterns learned during training. This creates three important challenges: knowledge can become stale, domain-specific information may not be present, and generated answers may contain hallucinated facts. RAG provides an alternative architecture in which external information is retrieved at query time and used to ground generation.

2. What is RAG?

Retrieval-Augmented Generation combines two major capabilities: information retrieval and language generation. Given a user query, the system first searches a knowledge source for relevant content. The selected passages are then placed into the model's context along with the original question. The LLM uses this evidence to produce the final response.

3. Basic RAG Architecture

A typical RAG pipeline contains five stages: (1) document collection, (2) preprocessing and chunking, (3) embedding and indexing, (4) query retrieval, and (5) context-aware generation. During indexing, documents are divided into manageable chunks and converted into vector representations. At query time, the question is embedded, similar chunks are retrieved, and the most useful context is passed to the generator.

4. Retrieval Methods

RAG systems commonly use lexical retrieval, dense vector retrieval, or hybrid retrieval. Lexical methods such as BM25 are effective when exact terms matter. Dense retrieval represents queries and documents as embeddings and searches for semantic similarity. Hybrid retrieval combines lexical and semantic signals. Modern systems may also apply reranking after initial retrieval so that a smaller set of highly relevant passages reaches the LLM.

5. Evolution of RAG Research

Early retrieval-augmented work demonstrated that language models could access explicit external knowledge rather than storing everything in parameters. REALM introduced a retriever into language-model pre-training. The 2020 RAG paper by Lewis et al. formalized a generation framework combining a sequence-to-sequence model with a dense index of Wikipedia. Later work such as Atlas showed that retrieval-augmented models can perform strongly on knowledge-intensive tasks with few examples. Survey research has since organized RAG into increasingly advanced and modular pipelines.

6. Benefits

The major benefits of RAG are easier knowledge updates, use of private or domain-specific documents, improved traceability, and reduced dependence on memorized model knowledge. Because the external knowledge store can be updated without retraining the entire LLM, RAG is attractive for enterprise assistants, academic search, documentation systems, and customer-support applications.

7. Limitations and Challenges

RAG does not automatically eliminate hallucinations. Poor chunking, weak retrieval, irrelevant documents, duplicated content, or an unreliable knowledge source can lead to incorrect answers. Context-window limits can also restrict how much evidence can be provided. Additional challenges include retrieval latency, indexing cost, access control, evaluation of factuality, and ensuring that the generated answer is actually supported by the retrieved evidence.

8. Evaluation

A useful RAG evaluation should separate retrieval quality from generation quality. Retrieval can be evaluated using measures such as recall and precision of relevant passages. Generation can be assessed for answer correctness, relevance, faithfulness to retrieved evidence, and citation quality. In production systems, latency, cost, user satisfaction, and failure rates are also important. Evaluating only the final text can hide whether an error originated in retrieval or generation.

9. Practical Applications

RAG is useful wherever an AI system must answer from a changing or specialized knowledge base. Examples include college information assistants, company policy assistants, technical documentation chatbots, research assistants, legal-document search, product support, and internal knowledge portals. A college-focused RAG system, for example, could retrieve information from regulations, circulars, department documents, and event notices before generating an answer.

10. Future Directions

Research is moving toward more adaptive retrieval, better reranking, multimodal knowledge sources, agentic retrieval, stronger evidence verification, and more reliable evaluation. Recent research also explores systems where retrieval and reasoning interact repeatedly instead of performing a single retrieve-then-generate step. The broader goal is to make AI responses not only fluent, but also evidence-aware, updateable, and easier to verify.

11. Conclusion

RAG is an important architecture for building reliable knowledge-intensive AI applications. Its central idea is simple: retrieve relevant external knowledge before generating an answer. In practice, however, high-quality RAG requires careful document preparation, retrieval design, context selection, generation, and evaluation. RAG should therefore be viewed not as a single model, but as a complete information pipeline connecting data sources, retrieval systems, and generative models.
